Social media is the first thing dropped when the business gets busy

Most businesses know social media matters, but it rarely feels as urgent as the work directly in front of them, so it is usually the first thing to slip.

  • Posting is inconsistent, active for a few weeks then silent for a month
  • Content gets created reactively, whenever someone remembers, rather than planned ahead
  • Comments and messages go unanswered for longer than they should
  • Nobody is reviewing what is actually working, so the same mistakes repeat

Inconsistency is the specific problem here, more than quality. A channel that posts reliably, even simply, tends to outperform one that posts brilliantly but unpredictably, because consistency is what an algorithm and an audience both reward.

Content planning and scheduling do not require a local presence

Planning a content calendar, scheduling posts and monitoring engagement all happen through the same platforms and tools regardless of where the manager is based. The work is entirely digital and system driven.

What it requires is a genuine understanding of your brand voice and audience, established during onboarding and refined over time, plus the discipline to maintain a consistent posting cadence rather than working in occasional bursts.

It also means content and community management happen on a schedule rather than whenever someone has a spare moment, which is precisely the consistency that most businesses are currently missing.

You are probably ready if this sounds familiar

Most businesses reach this point once social media has been inconsistent for long enough to notice.

  • Your last post was more than a week ago and nobody has a plan for the next one
  • Content gets created the same day it is meant to post, if at all
  • Comments and messages sometimes go unanswered for days
  • You do not know which posts are actually working and which are not

None of these signs mean your content is bad. They reflect the simple reality that nobody currently owns social media as a consistent, ongoing responsibility.

Scales with the number of channels and campaigns you run

Most businesses start with one or two core channels, getting posting cadence and quality consistent before adding more. Community management is often added once content planning is running smoothly.

As you add channels or run more campaigns, the same social media manager can generally absorb more before a second is needed, since the underlying process does not change, only the volume and channel count.

Larger businesses sometimes add a second specialist focused specifically on paid campaigns or a particular channel, once that area is significant enough to warrant dedicated focus.

Common questions

Can the manager create original graphics and video?

Depends on the scope agreed. Some engagements include content creation, others focus on planning, scheduling and community management using content you supply.

Will the content sound like it was written by our business?

That is the goal of onboarding, establishing your brand voice clearly before content goes live, and refining it based on your feedback.

Can this role manage paid social campaigns too?

That can be included in scoping if relevant, though many engagements start with organic content and add paid management later.

How is engagement handled outside business hours?

Working hours are agreed to align with your needs. Some businesses want coverage outside standard hours, which is factored into scoping.

What if we do not have a clear brand voice defined yet?

That is common, and part of onboarding is working through it together rather than assuming it already exists.
