Nobody owns the process, so it depends entirely on you

Most growing businesses have workflows that exist but are not really managed, which means they only run well when the owner is paying attention.

  • Processes work when you are watching them and drift when you are not
  • Reporting exists but nobody is proactively reviewing it for problems
  • Escalations come straight to you because nobody else has the authority or context to handle them
  • You are the single point of failure for operational consistency

This is a different problem to simply needing more hands. It is a gap in ownership: tasks are being done, but nobody besides you is accountable for whether the whole process actually works.

Process ownership is a skill set, not a location requirement

Managing a workflow, reviewing reporting and handling escalations within defined authority are all tasks built on judgement applied to information, not on physical presence. A capable operations manager can own these processes from anywhere with the right access and context.

What matters is that the person genuinely understands your workflow and has the authority to act within agreed boundaries, rather than needing to check with you at every step. That authority is established during scoping, not assumed.

This also creates real continuity. A process that is actively owned and monitored does not silently degrade the way one that only gets attention when something breaks tends to.

  • Reporting exists but nobody is reviewing it proactively for problems

None of these signs mean your team is underperforming. They reflect a gap in ownership specifically, which is a different problem to simply needing more hands doing tasks.

Scales with the number of processes you need owned

Most businesses start with one critical workflow, the one causing the most friction, and expand from there. The operations manager typically earns broader authority as trust is established.

As the business adds more processes, the same operations manager can often take on ownership of additional workflows before a second is needed, provided the processes are related enough to manage coherently.

Larger operations sometimes split ownership across two operations managers once the process portfolio is broad enough that one person managing all of it would be stretched too thin.

Common questions

How much authority does this role actually have?

Exactly what you agree to during scoping. Authority is built up deliberately, not assumed, and can expand as trust is established.

Is this different from an operations assistant?

Yes. An assistant executes coordination tasks. A manager owns outcomes and has defined decision making authority within the process.

What happens with decisions outside their authority?

They are escalated to you through an agreed process, so nothing outside the boundary is decided without your input.

Can this role manage other team members?

In some engagements, yes, particularly where the operations manager is coordinating other offshore team members on the same workflow.

How is performance measured for this role?

Against the KPIs and process outcomes agreed during scoping, reviewed on a regular cadence you set.
