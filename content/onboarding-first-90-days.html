Bad onboarding is why offshore engagements fail, not the model itself

Most offshore hiring disappointments trace back to onboarding, not to the person or the model. A team member left to sink or swim never becomes fully effective. A team member who is never handed real ownership stays a cost rather than becoming a genuine asset.

  • No structured plan, so the first weeks are improvised rather than deliberate
  • The owner ends up doing all the training personally, which defeats the point of hiring support
  • Access and systems are set up piecemeal, so the person spends week one blocked rather than working
  • Nobody defines what success looks like, so performance is judged on vague impressions later

A structured 90 day framework exists specifically to prevent these failure points, not as a formality.

Trust and ownership build in stages, not on day one

It would be tempting to hand a new team member full ownership immediately and hope for the best. In practice, that approach produces mistakes that were avoidable, simply because the person had not yet built enough context about your business to make good judgement calls.

A staged approach front loads context building, expands responsibility as competence is demonstrated, and only hands over full ownership once both sides have evidence it is warranted, not just optimism that it will work out.

This also protects you. A mistake made in week one on a narrow, supervised task is cheap. The same mistake made in month three on a broadly delegated task is expensive.

The first 30 days are about context, not output

System access is set up before day one wherever possible, so the person is not blocked waiting on logins during their first week. Introductions are made to anyone they will need to work with regularly.

Work in this period is closely defined and closely supervised. The goal is not maximum output, it is building accurate context about how your business actually operates, which is what everything afterward depends on.

  • System access and tools configured and tested before the start date
  • Documented processes provided for the initial scope of the role
  • Daily or near daily check ins to catch misunderstandings early, while they are still cheap to fix
  • A narrow, well defined set of tasks rather than broad responsibility

Responsibility grows as competence is demonstrated

By this stage, the team member has handled the initial scope consistently and understands your systems and expectations. Scope typically expands here, and supervision typically reduces, though not disappears.

This is also the point where gaps become visible, whether in the person's approach or in how the role itself was originally scoped. Adjusting the scope now is normal and expected, not a sign anything has gone wrong.

  • Additional tasks added to the original scope
  • Check ins move from daily to a few times a week
  • The team member starts making some judgement calls independently, within agreed boundaries
  • Any scope adjustments are made based on real experience, not assumptions from the original scoping call

By day ninety, the role should run without you managing every step

This is the point where the team member should be operating with real ownership of their scope, and where a genuine performance baseline can be established, since enough time has passed to see a stable pattern rather than early settling in noise.

A formal check in at the 90 day mark is standard practice, reviewing what is working, what needs adjustment, and whether the original scope still matches what the business actually needs.

  • The role operates with agreed independence, within the boundaries set during scoping
  • Regular check ins shift to a standing cadence, typically weekly, rather than being reactive
  • A performance baseline is established, giving you a real standard to measure against going forward
  • Any longer term scope changes are discussed and agreed at this point

Onboarding works best when both sides show up for it

This is not a passive process on your end. The clients who get the most out of the first 90 days are the ones who invest real time early, because that investment is what shortens the path to full ownership.

  • Be available for the initial check ins, particularly in the first 30 days
  • Give honest feedback early, since small corrections now are cheaper than large ones later
  • Provide access to the systems and information the role genuinely needs, rather than a restricted version that slows things down
  • Treat the team member as part of the team from day one, not as a trial to be evaluated at arm's length

Common questions

What if the role is not working out by day 90?

Clear engagement terms and replacement support are provided in the service agreement. The 90 day check in is specifically designed to surface problems early, well before they become a bigger issue.

Can the scope change significantly during onboarding?

Yes, and it often does. The original scoping call is a starting point, not a fixed contract. Adjustments based on real experience are normal and expected.

Who runs the check ins during onboarding?

Your named ACS contact, in coordination with you and the team member. You are not left managing onboarding alone.

Does onboarding look different for different roles?

The broad structure is the same, but the specific content differs. A telesales role reaches independence on calls faster than an operations manager reaches full delegated authority, for example.

What happens after day 90?

The role moves into standard ongoing performance and reporting, covered on a separate page, since onboarding is complete and normal operation begins.
